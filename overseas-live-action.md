# Premium international live-action short drama

Read this reference for overseas-market projects, non-Chinese dialogue, or premium international quality requests.

## Market brief

Lock or explicitly assume:

- target territory and audience segment;
- dialogue language, regional variety, accent policy, and subtitle language;
- platform, aspect ratio, episode length, rating, and genre promise;
- cultural setting, period, socioeconomic world, and production scale;
- hook deadline, episode turn, cliffhanger type, and next-episode promise.

Never treat “overseas” or “Western” as one culture. A contemporary Los Angeles workplace, London family home, Lagos nightlife venue, and rural Australian town require different speech, institutions, casting, design, and behavior.

## Adaptation test

For every scene, separate:

1. **Story engine:** desire, obstacle, reversal, reveal, emotional outcome. Preserve unless the user approves a story change.
2. **Cultural mechanism:** how status, money, romance, family, work, law, medicine, school, or reputation operate. Adapt to the target market.
3. **Surface detail:** names, food, signage, phones, vehicles, clothing, architecture, currency, units, and slang. Make these coherent, not decorative.

When the script is locked, do not translate or adapt it. Read dialogue aloud only to calculate duration and clip boundaries. Report unnatural or historically inconsistent wording as a risk without altering it.

## Premium visual benchmark

Define a show-specific visual bible instead of requesting generic “cinematic quality”:

- capture format and aspect ratio;
- lens family and depth-of-field behavior;
- camera height, stability, and movement rules;
- contrast, saturation, white balance, skin-tone treatment, highlight roll-off, and grain;
- motivated key, fill logic, practical fixtures, negative fill, and time-of-day continuity;
- production-design palette, materials, wear, clutter, signage, and socioeconomic signals;
- wardrobe silhouette, fabric, fit, grooming, makeup, and episode-level continuity.

For daylight scenes, include a physical light diagram in the visual bible: sun azimuth and elevation, sky condition, subject orientation, camera axis, bounce surfaces, negative fill, exposure priority, facial lighting ratio, cast-shadow direction, and expected hair transmission. Backlit hair may carry a fine natural rim while the face remains darker; do not “correct” the face with unsupported frontal light.

Use references to extract attributes such as coverage density, contrast, palette, performance restraint, or sound perspective. Do not ask for a living filmmaker's exact style or reproduce protected characters and performers.

## Performance and dialogue

- Give each beat a playable verb and subtext, then express it through gaze, breath, posture, jaw, hands, distance, and timing.
- Analyze each exact line for what is said, what is meant, what is withheld, who the thought is directed toward, and where the thought turns. Convert that analysis into a timed facial-performance instruction rather than an adjective such as “angry” or “shocked.”
- For each close performance shot, specify only motivated facial details: focus and saccades, blink placement, eyelid aperture, brow and glabella tension, cheek tone, nostril response, jaw, lip corners, swallow, breath, asymmetry, onset, peak, release, and residual expression. Keep the face compatible with the exact spoken phonemes.
- For each spoken line, specify the emotional state at entry, the exact verbal pivot, the body-language response to that pivot, and the physical endpoint. Include culturally and historically plausible hand gestures, head movement, posture, and interpersonal distance; do not reduce performance to lip-sync.
- Keep gesture frequency human. Use a gesture because the thought changes, the speaker emphasizes or rejects something, or status shifts—not because the character is speaking. Let hands rest naturally between motivated movements.
- Favor screen-scale behavior. Remove stage projection, constant eyebrow acting, decorative smirks, and uniformly intense delivery.
- Bind every spoken line to one character and one visible mouth. Name all visible nonspeakers and require closed, relaxed mouths.
- Define voice identity separately from the temporary emotion: territory, age impression, register, rhythm, pitch range, texture, articulation, and code-switch rules.
- Avoid accent caricature. Never request imitation of a recognizable actor or public figure.

## Coverage and generation strategy

- Generate fragile dialogue coverage as separate nodes: geography anchor, speaker singles, listener reactions, and inserts.
- Plan dialogue and internal monologue as alternating editorial coverage, not as a single generated performance. For dialogue, return to the speaker at verbal pivots and use listeners during evolving reactions. For voice-over, keep lips closed and let the uninterrupted thought cross relevant faces, evidence, props, and environment before returning to the thinker.
- Use a validated character reference set: neutral face, three-quarter view, full body, wardrobe, and controlled expression range. Keep identity anchors separate from transient action.
- Use accepted footage or its actual final frame as the source of truth for the next connected shot.
- Prefer image-to-video or start-frame control for exact composition and continuity. Use end-frame control when a handoff, match cut, or motion endpoint must land precisely.
- Reserve continuous extensions for simple sustained action or establishing movement. Do not extend through speaker changes, complex hand contact, wardrobe changes, or geography resets.
- Budget a clean editorial handle at the head and tail when the tool and workflow allow it.

## Sound standard

Specify dialogue, room tone, environmental perspective, prop Foley, cloth movement, transitions, and score separately. Dialogue must remain intelligible on a phone speaker without sounding detached from the room. Preserve acoustic perspective across matching angles. Use silence only when it changes power, meaning, or anticipation.

Build ambience in perspective-correct layers. Distant traffic or pedestrian murmur may be audible through windows and doors, but background speech must remain indistinct and must not introduce names, facts, or plot. Lower ambience under principal dialogue instead of muting the world completely.

## Quality gate

Mark each category `pass`, `revise`, or `regenerate`:

1. Story clarity and hook strength.
2. Target-market authenticity and idiomatic dialogue.
3. Casting and identity consistency.
4. Performance specificity and emotional progression.
5. Lip-sync, speaker assignment, and voice continuity.
6. Geography, eyeline, action, prop, wardrobe, light, and time continuity.
7. Anatomy, hands, contact physics, reflections, shadows, text, and background behavior.
8. Composition, coverage, edit rhythm, and vertical safe zones when applicable.
9. Production design, material realism, skin texture, and absence of synthetic gloss.
10. Dialogue mix, ambience, Foley, music restraint, and transition cleanliness.

Regenerate identity, lip-sync, anatomy, physics, or story failures. Patch or edit isolated background, timing, mix, or cosmetic defects only when the repair will not create a continuity mismatch. Rank fixes by audience visibility and story impact.

## Reference practice

When the user asks to reference overseas work, build a small benchmark set by purpose rather than copying one title: one reference for narrative rhythm, one for performance, one for lighting and production design, one for camera and edit grammar, and one for sound. Cite named, current examples only after verifying them. Convert each reference into observable attributes and explain what will and will not be borrowed.
